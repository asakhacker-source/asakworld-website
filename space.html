Space Systems & Infrastructure

Understand the connected launch, spacecraft, ground and operations systems that make space missions useful.

Launch systems connect Earth to orbit

A launch vehicle carries a payload through ascent and delivers it to a planned trajectory. Launch is not an isolated event: ground crews prepare the vehicle and payload, tracking systems observe the flight, and mission teams take over when the spacecraft separates. The chosen trajectory, communications plan and initial operating sequence determine how the vehicle on Earth becomes an operational asset in space.

Spacecraft and satellites

A spacecraft usually combines a bus with a payload. The bus provides the common services needed to operate: structure, electrical power, thermal control, communications, navigation support and onboard computing. The payload performs the mission-specific work, such as measuring a signal, relaying communications, observing Earth or conducting a scientific experiment. Satellites are spacecraft designed to operate in orbit, but the same division between platform and payload applies to many missions.

Payloads and mission purpose

A communications payload handles signals between users or networks. A navigation payload provides carefully managed timing and signal information. Sensing payloads may collect imagery or other measurements, while science instruments are designed around a particular observation. The payload defines what a mission can learn or provide, but its requirements also influence the bus: an instrument may need stable pointing, a particular thermal environment, storage capacity or regular opportunities to return data.

Power, thermal control and communications

Spacecraft generate or store electrical energy, distribute it to subsystems and protect essential functions when power is constrained. Thermal control manages the fact that electronics and instruments must operate through changing exposure to sunlight and deep cold. Communications links carry commands to the spacecraft, telemetry about its health back to mission teams, and payload data to ground systems. Relays and ground networks can extend contact opportunities, but every link is planned around distance, orientation, available power and the mission’s data needs.

Navigation, guidance and sensing

Navigation establishes where a spacecraft is and how it is moving. Attitude determination establishes its orientation, which is important for antennas, instruments, solar arrays and manoeuvres. Sensors, onboard estimation and control systems combine measurements with a mission plan to keep the vehicle pointed and operating as intended. Earth observation turns light or other physical signals into measurements that can support imaging, mapping, environmental observation and scientific analysis after careful processing.

Ground stations and mission control

Ground infrastructure tracks spacecraft, schedules contacts, sends commands, receives telemetry and processes returned data. Mission control combines these activities with procedures for normal operation and unexpected conditions. A useful space service often depends as much on calibration, data handling and distribution on Earth as on the instrument in orbit. Ground systems provide the evidence that lets operators decide whether a spacecraft is healthy and whether a measurement is ready for use.

Orbital operations and autonomy

Operations continue for the life of a mission. Teams plan activities, watch spacecraft health, maintain an orbit when needed and consider collision-awareness information. Onboard software can assist with fault detection, scheduling, navigation and data handling when communications are limited. That autonomy is usually bounded: it follows tested rules, preserves safe options and returns information to people rather than replacing mission accountability.

Reliability, exploration and science

Redundancy, fault tolerance, testing, monitoring and graceful degradation help a mission continue when a component or link behaves unexpectedly. Recovery plans give operators choices when time or power is limited. Planetary and science missions may face longer communications delays, demanding environments and long-lived instruments. Their design therefore connects robust power and thermal planning with autonomous safeguards, careful data handling and a clear scientific purpose.

How space infrastructure connects

The system chain runs from launch to spacecraft, payload, onboard systems, communications, ground infrastructure, mission operations and finally the data or application layer. A rocket alone cannot create a service, and a satellite alone cannot interpret its observations. Space technology works when each layer shares realistic requirements, records what it did and supports the people who use the resulting information.
