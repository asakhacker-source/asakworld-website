Animation & Immersive Systems

Understand how assets, motion, rendering and interactive systems connect in a modern production pipeline.

What an animation pipeline contains

Animation production moves an idea through a chain of connected decisions. A concept establishes the story, visual language and shot requirements. Artists then create assets, prepare them for movement, animate or simulate the action, light the scene, render frames and combine those frames into a finished delivery. The pipeline matters because each stage depends on usable information from the previous one: a late asset change can affect a rig, a shot, lighting and the final composite.

2D and 3D production workflows

In 2D work, boards, layouts, drawings, timing, cleanup, colour and compositing establish motion one image at a time or through reusable elements. In 3D work, artists build geometry, define materials, place cameras and lights, then animate a virtual scene. Both workflows need planning, editorial review and careful timing. The difference is mainly where the visual information lives: drawn layers in 2D, or a spatial scene with reusable assets in 3D.

Assets, modelling and materials

An asset can be a character, prop, environment, effect or sound-ready scene element. Modelling gives a 3D asset its form; textures and materials describe surface colour, roughness, transparency and other visible qualities. Scene assets need sensible scale, naming and organisation so lighting, simulation and other departments can use them without rebuilding work. Asset libraries save time only when their versions and usage rights are clear.

Rigging and motion

Rigging places a skeleton and controls inside a model so an animator can pose and move it. Keyframes record chosen positions or values over time, while interpolation creates motion between those choices. Motion-capture data can provide a performance starting point, but it usually needs cleanup and artistic adjustment. Whether motion begins from a drawing, a rig or captured data, the final result depends on timing, weight, contact and the intention of the shot.

Lighting, rendering and compositing

Lighting directs attention, describes shape and helps a scene feel consistent from shot to shot. Rendering converts authored scene data into frames by calculating the interaction of cameras, materials, lights and effects. Compositing then combines rendered passes, matte work, colour adjustments and other elements into the delivered image. Separating these passes gives teams more control, but it also requires shared colour management and clear review decisions.

Real-time graphics and simulation

Offline rendering can spend substantial time producing a frame; real-time engines must respond within a frame budget while someone interacts with the scene. That constraint shapes geometry, textures, lighting and effects. It makes real-time graphics useful for games, interactive visualisation, design previews and virtual production. Simulation and procedural systems add physics-driven motion, particles, crowds, environments or variations generated from rules. They reduce repetitive setup, but artists still set constraints and choose results that serve the work.

AI assistance, virtual production and immersive media

AI-assisted tools can help organise assets, explore early ideas, process motion or accelerate repetitive workflow steps. They do not replace the full production pipeline, editorial judgement or responsibility for source material. Virtual production connects real-time environments with cameras and performances. AR and VR add tracking, input, display timing, comfort and accessibility requirements, so an interactive experience needs different testing from a linear film.

How the complete pipeline connects

Concept leads to asset creation, then rigging, animation, lighting, rendering, compositing and delivery. Real-time workflows can shorten the loop by allowing lighting, camera and performance decisions to be reviewed together, but they still depend on prepared assets and clear technical limits. A strong production process makes those dependencies visible early rather than treating each department as isolated.
