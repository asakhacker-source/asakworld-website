Vehicle & Mobility Systems

Understand how energy, mechanics, electronics, software and infrastructure combine in a modern vehicle system.

Vehicle technology as a connected system

Modern vehicles combine mechanical structures with electrical power, electronics, embedded computing, sensing, communications and software. These layers exchange information continuously: an energy system provides power to propulsion and computing, sensors inform control systems, and software coordinates functions across the vehicle. Looking at one feature alone can hide the dependencies that determine how a vehicle behaves in ordinary use and under fault conditions.

Electric powertrains

An electric powertrain links energy storage, power electronics, an electric motor, drivetrain components and control electronics. The battery supplies electrical energy; an inverter manages power delivered to the motor; and control software coordinates requested propulsion with operating limits. Mechanical systems still matter because torque reaches the road through a drivetrain, tyres and chassis. The design task is therefore a conversation between electrical, mechanical and software requirements.

Battery packs and battery management

A battery pack groups cells into modules and assemblies that can be monitored and protected. A battery-management system observes conditions, estimates operating state, balances cells where appropriate and coordinates protection responses. It also works with thermal systems because temperature affects what the pack and charging system can safely do. These functions make the battery a managed energy system rather than a passive component.

Charging architecture

Charging connects external infrastructure to a vehicle interface, power-conversion equipment and the battery-management system. The vehicle and charger communicate about the requested session, while onboard systems manage conversion and charging control according to the vehicle’s conditions. Site capacity, connector compatibility, billing and maintenance belong to the wider charging experience even though they sit outside the vehicle.

Sensors, assistance and automation

Cameras, radar and other sensors observe the environment, while position and motion sensing helps establish how the vehicle itself is moving. Driver-assistance systems conceptually link sensors to perception, localisation or context, planning or decision support, and vehicle control. Assistance is not the same as higher automation: systems have operating boundaries, and safe use depends on clear feedback, appropriate supervision and a realistic response when information is uncertain.

Embedded computing and vehicle networks

Processors and controllers support powertrain control, body systems, infotainment, sensing, driver assistance and diagnostics. They need internal networks to exchange control messages, sensor data and status information. These networks coordinate separate controllers so a vehicle can respond as one system rather than a collection of isolated modules. Diagnostics make it possible to observe faults and service behaviour without treating every subsystem as opaque.

Connectivity and software-defined vehicles

Vehicle-to-cloud or service connectivity can support updates, diagnostics, navigation and fleet or maintenance services. It also creates security and privacy responsibilities, because access to a vehicle function or record must be controlled. A software-defined vehicle describes hardware capabilities increasingly coordinated and extended through software. This can involve central or domain computing, separation of hardware and software functions, and updateable applications—provided changes are tested, authenticated and understandable.

Power, thermal management and reliability

Electrical and thermal management run across the battery, power electronics, motors, computing hardware and cabin or system loads. Heat must be moved or controlled so components remain within their intended operating conditions. Functional safety and reliability work adds fault detection, diagnostic coverage, redundancy where justified, safe-state behaviour and validation. The purpose is not to promise that faults never occur, but to make faults observable and to limit their consequences.

Mobility infrastructure and the complete system

Vehicles interact with charging sites, roads, navigation services, digital infrastructure and fleet or mobility platforms. The conceptual chain runs from energy source to power electronics, propulsion, sensing, embedded compute, control, vehicle networks and connectivity services. These systems operate together: a software update may affect a controller, a sensor fault may change assistance behaviour, and charging availability can shape how the energy system is used.
